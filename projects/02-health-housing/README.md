## PLACES

**A Number That Was Never Measured.**

`D04_health_prevalence/D04_health_prevalence.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Distinguish a modelled interval from a sampling margin empirically, not by reading the documentation — by measuring how each scales with population.
- Separate reliability from rank identifiability, and see a measure clear the conventional publishing threshold while still supporting no ranking whatsoever.
- Replace an impossible estimand with possible ones — detection, tiers, exceedance — and quantify what each actually yields.
- Bound the new information in a ranking by asking how much of it survives without the modelled source at all.

**Sources** — American Community Survey (acs/acs5); PLACES county estimates, 2025 release, crude prevalence  
3 provenance records committed in `D04_health_prevalence/provenance/`.

**Limitations**

- One state. Every figure here is Virginia's 133 counties and independent cities. Handbook §7.2 runs the same measurements across all 2,956 US counties and reaches the same qualitative result, so the *conclusions* generalise; the *magnitudes* here do not.
- Four measures of forty. They were chosen for contrast, not sampled. §7.2 shows the size artifact and the covariate-recoverability diagnostic both varying by an order of magnitude across just these four — so neither may be inherited from the source, the geography, or a neighbouring measure. The other 36 in this release are unmeasured.
- The standard errors are recovered, not published. PLACES publishes an interval; the `se` used throughout assumes it is symmetric about the estimate. It is mildly asymmetric, and the provenance record carries the worst asymmetry observed in the pull. Every §3–§5 result inherits that approximation.

## Fair Market Rents

**When the Number Is a Decision.**

`D05_housing_fair_market_rents/D05_fair_market_rents.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Distinguish a published number's *definition* from the *quantity it is published as*, and measure the gap rather than assuming it away.
- Quantify what a statutory geography costs: how much variation a single binding number is required to ignore, separated from the level shift that would otherwise mask it.
- Detect that a publisher's machine-readable distribution is not the operative schedule, by checking it against the authoritative files rather than trusting its label.
- Recover a revision that has no revision series, by differencing two publications of the same fiscal year.

**Sources** — Census ACS; BLS LAUS; FRED / ALFRED; HUD FMR; CDC PLACES

**Limitations**

- One bedroom size. Everything is reported for 2-bedroom units. §5 shows bedroom sizes move differently, so the §1 and §2 magnitudes should not be assumed to hold at 0 or 4.
- 65% ACS coverage. New England FMR areas are town-level and drop out of every FMR-versus-ACS statistic. Those areas are disproportionately in a few states, so the coverage loss is not geographically neutral.
- ACS margins are carried but not propagated. `acs_moe` is retrieved and unused. The §2 dispersion statistics are computed on point estimates, and a county whose median rent has a wide margin contributes to the spread as though it were known. Exercise 4.

## Home Values

**Why a Median Cannot Be Averaged.**

`D05_housing_home_values/D05_home_values.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Know which published statistics are aggregable and which are not. Counts add. Medians do not, under any weighting.
- Reconstruct a median from a pooled bracket distribution, and validate the reconstruction against the publisher's own figure.
- Check that two geography levels of one API cover the same universe before comparing an aggregate of one against the other.
- Notice the geographies where the statistic does not exist, rather than dropping them.

**Sources** — Census ACS; CDC PLACES

**Limitations**

- One vintage. Everything here is ACS 5-year 2023. The bracket edges are nominal dollars and are not re-based between vintages, so bracket counts across years are not comparable without deflation and the `$2,000,000 or more` bracket fills over time.
- No margin on the reconstruction. Section 6's method reproduces the published median and cannot produce a confidence interval for it. The Bureau computes median MOEs from replicate weights, which this table does not carry.
- The top bracket is open. `median_from_brackets` raises on an unidentified percentile. No geography here has its median inside the open bracket, but a high-value tract-level analysis could, and this notebook does not exercise that path.

## CHAS

**What a Tabulation Loses.**

`D05_housing_needs_chas/D05_chas_custom_tabulation.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Quantify what a derived product discards by recovering the source's published uncertainty for the same quantity, rather than noting its absence in prose.
- Distinguish agreement from equivalence — two figures can match and still not be the same kind of claim.
- Match reference periods before comparing, when a derived product lags its source.
- Handle suppression that arrives as an absence rather than as a flag or a sentinel.

**Sources** — Census ACS; BLS QCEW; HUD FMR; HUD CHAS

**Limitations**

- One state. Every figure is Virginia. The suppression rate, the index duplication and the dictionary errors are properties of the publisher and should generalise; the *magnitudes* in §1 and §2 are not claimed to.
- One quantity for the margin comparison. §1 uses renter cost burden over 30%, because it is the quantity CHAS and `B25070` most nearly share. A different column could carry a different margin, and this does not establish a corpus-wide figure.
- The correspondence is close, not exact. CHAS and `B25070` are built from the same microdata but not by the same recipe — CHAS's universe excludes some households `B25070` retains. The comparison is reported as agreement within a margin, never as an identity.

## Food Security

**A County Column That Is a State Value.**

`D17_food_security/D17_food_security.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Test the grain of every column before using it, rather than trusting the row identifier.
- Recognise that a file can mix geographies silently, and that adjacent columns need not share one.
- Compute an effective sample size from distinct values rather than row count.
- See what a fictional n does to a p-value, on real data.

**Sources** — CDC PLACES; USDA ERS; Zillow

**Limitations**

- The classifier detects replication, not its cause. A column constant within every state may be a state estimate copied down, a state-level policy variable that is genuinely constant within a state, or a modelling artifact. Section 4 establishes the first; the soda-tax variables are almost certainly the second, and being correctly constant does not make them county measurements.
- "State-replicated" is not "wrong". A state SNAP participation rate is a real number. The defect is the row geography it is served at, and the inferences that invites.
- Effective sample size is bounded by distinct values, not equal to them. √(n/k) is an approximation for the standard-error inflation, exact only under equal group sizes. It is used here as an order of magnitude.

## Subjective Well-Being

**The One Notebook That Should Be Simulated.**

`D30_subjective_wellbeing/D30_subjective_wellbeing.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Score an analysis rather than read it, by working where the data-generating process is declared and the true coefficients are known by construction.
- Separate a bad result from a bad method. Measure how often a design produces a false finding before concluding that a particular false finding means something.
- Read a coefficient's standard error as the finding, and see two real effects at the same n, in the same model, recovered with completely different reliability.
- Recognise a performance figure that is a property of the split, not of the model.

**Sources** — Census ACS

**Limitations**

- Nothing here is a fact about well-being. Every number in this notebook is a property of a generator. It supports conclusions about *methods*, and about the archived artifact, and about nothing else.
- One data-generating process. All rates are specific to this design: four predictors, two of them null, n = 50, Gaussian and independent, α = 0.05. They will move under collinearity, heavier tails, or a different predictor count. The method of measuring them generalises; the numbers do not.
- The predictors here are independent. Real socioeconomic predictors are strongly correlated, which makes coefficient recovery worse than shown, not better. Section 5's spread is a floor.
