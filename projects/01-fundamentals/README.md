## Income and Poverty

**Measuring Inequality with ACS Data.**

`D01_income_and_poverty/D01_income_and_poverty.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Retrieve household income, poverty and educational attainment estimates from the Census ACS API, with the vintage pinned and the provenance of every column recorded.
- Recognise the two failure modes that silently corrupt public-data pipelines: a success status carrying an error page, and jam values that look like numbers.
- Construct a Lorenz curve and compute a Gini coefficient from published income brackets, and state the assumptions that choice requires.
- Validate your computed Gini against the Census's own published index — an external check that tells you whether your pipeline is right, not merely self-consistent.

**Sources** — Census ACS

**Limitations**

- Survey uncertainty is propagated for the target, not for everything. Margins of error are retrieved and carried through the poverty rate, and the notebook reports how much of its own ranking the survey actually resolves. They are not carried through the Gini or the regression coefficients, both of which would need a replicate-weight approach the public API does not expose. D03 develops the method in full.
- Period estimates. 5-year ACS figures describe a 2019–2023 window, not a point in time. They are not a snapshot and should not be differenced against single-year data.
- Gini from brackets is an approximation. Two stated assumptions — within-bracket uniformity and an assumed open-top mean — bias the result. The sensitivity sweep quantifies the second; the first is not quantified here.

## Employment

**A Rate That Falls for Two Opposite Reasons.**

`D02_employment_and_labor/D02_employment_and_labor.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Verify a published accounting identity rather than assuming it, and use it to establish what a rate's numerator and denominator actually are.
- Decompose a change in a ratio into movement of its numerator and its denominator, and identify the cases where they imply opposite conclusions.
- Recognise model-based small-area estimates: what "controlled to the state total" means, how to test for it, and why it makes sub-state figures non-independent.
- Work without margins of error — state what cannot be concluded when the publisher supplies no uncertainty at all, rather than silently treating point estimates as exact.

**Sources** — American Community Survey (acs/acs5); BLS timeseries API v2  
3 provenance records committed in `D02_employment_and_labor/provenance/`.

**Limitations**

- LAUS publishes no measure of uncertainty, so nothing in this notebook can say whether two states' LAUS rates are distinguishable. That is not a gap this analysis can fill by computing something; the publisher does not provide the input.
- LAUS sub-state estimates are model output controlled to a state total. They are not independent observations, so variance estimates, regression standard errors and pairwise tests across counties are not supported by the data as published.
- LAUS is revised annually. Each benchmarking cycle rewrites history; figures retrieved on a later date will differ from these. The retrieval date is in the provenance record for that reason.

## Educational Attainment

**When Is a Difference Real?.**

`D03_education/D03_education.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Retrieve ACS estimates together with their published margins of error (`_M`).
- Aggregate several count estimates into one, combining their margins in quadrature rather than by addition.
- Propagate a margin of error through a derived percentage using the Census's proportion formula, including the documented fallback when its radicand goes negative.
- Test whether two geographies are statistically distinguishable, using the Census's published Z-test rather than eyeballing whether intervals overlap.

**Sources** — Census ACS

**Limitations**

- Independence is assumed, and bounded. The Census aggregation formula treats the four attainment categories as independent; they are drawn from the same respondents, so they are not. Rather than assert the approximation is adequate, the notebook recomputes every margin under perfect positive correlation — the worst possible case — and checks whether the headline conclusion survives. It does. The conclusion therefore does not rest on the assumption.
- Pairwise tests are corrected, not merely caveated. Holm–Bonferroni (family-wise) and Benjamini–Hochberg (false discovery rate) are both applied and reported. The corrected counts are the ones to quote. Which procedure is appropriate depends on whether a single false claim is costly (Holm) or a bounded proportion is tolerable (BH).
- 90%, not 95%. Every interval here is a 90% interval, the ACS convention. They are narrower than the 95% intervals most readers assume.

## Consumer Spending

**The Number You Acted On Is Not the Number in the Database.**

`D08_consumer_spending/D08_consumer_spending.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Retrieve a series as it stood on a past date (an ALFRED vintage) as well as currently, and explain why the two differ.
- Distinguish rebasing from revision using the cross-vintage level ratio, and state which comparisons each one invalidates.
- Quantify revision in the terms a decision-maker uses — not the level, but the growth rate, and whether its sign changed.
- Account for publication lag: the data available on a date is shorter as well as different.

**Sources** — FRED series PCE; FRED series PCEC96  
4 provenance records committed in `D08_consumer_spending/provenance/`.

**Limitations**

- One vintage pair. Everything here compares a single past publication date with today. The full revision path runs through hundreds of intermediate vintages, and the largest revision for a given month may sit at neither endpoint.
- The rebasing test is a diagnostic, not a proof. A near-constant ratio is consistent with rebasing; it would also arise from any uniform multiplicative correction. The threshold is a declared convention, not a property of the data.
- Growth is computed month over month on a SAAR series. That is a change in annualised pace, not a monthly or an annual growth rate, and it is not comparable to a year-over-year figure.
