## Difference-in-Differences

**Medicaid Expansion and the Uninsured Rate.**

`C01_medicaid_expansion_did/C01_medicaid_expansion_did.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- State a difference-in-differences estimand, and say which assumption stands between the estimate and a causal reading of it.
- Compute a two-by-two difference-in-differences by hand from four group means, and show that a two-way fixed-effects regression returns the same number.
- Read an event-study plot, and explain why the leads — not the lags — are the argument.
- Run two falsification tests (a joint test of pre-period coefficients and a placebo outcome), and apply a decision rule that was written down before the data were seen.

**Sources** — American Community Survey (acs/acs1)  
13 provenance records committed in `C01_medicaid_expansion_did/provenance/`.

**Limitations**

- The 2014 cohort. The design uses only states that expanded on 1 January 2014, against states that never expanded. That makes the timing single and the regression clean, at a cost: the states that expanded part-way through 2014 and the later adopters are not used here (the sample step prints how many). Later adopters are handled with staggered-timing methods in C03.
- What is being compared. The contrast is expansion against the ACA as it applied in the states that did not expand, not expansion against nothing: the marketplaces, subsidies and the mandate applied everywhere from 2014, and in non-expansion states people between 100% and 138% of the poverty level became eligible for marketplace subsidies. The control states also start with a higher uninsured rate (printed above), so a 2014 shock that scales with the level would shrink an absolute ATT toward zero, and the lead test cannot detect it. The added log-outcome check shows the proportional version.
- Contaminated groups. Wisconsin is a control state but covered adults up to the poverty level from April 2014. Delaware, the District of Columbia, Massachusetts, New York and Vermont already had comprehensive adult coverage before 2014, and California, Connecticut, Minnesota, New Jersey and Washington had early expansion options in 2010-2013, all in the treated group. An added leave-out check is above.

## Regression Discontinuity

**Health Insurance Coverage at Age 65.**

`C02_medicare_age65_rdd/C02_medicare_age65_rdd.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Explain why a threshold rule such as an eligibility age behaves like an experiment, and what has to be true for that to hold.
- Estimate a jump at a threshold with a local-linear fit and a triangular kernel, and show the estimate against the binned means it summarises.
- Report how the answer moves with the bandwidth instead of presenting one bandwidth as the answer.
- Run three falsification tests — a balance check on the running variable, placebo cutoffs and covariate continuity — and apply a decision rule written before the data were seen.

**Sources** — American Community Survey Public Use Microdata Sample (acs/acs1/pums)  
154 provenance records committed in `C02_medicare_age65_rdd/provenance/`.

**Limitations**

- Local to 65. The estimate is the jump at one threshold. It says nothing about the effect of coverage at any other age, or about the people far from 65.
- A discrete running variable. Age is reported in whole years, so the nearest observations are a year apart and the fit rests on a handful of age cells per side. The conventional interval uses the heteroskedasticity-robust variance over those few cells, which understates the uncertainty, and it ignores the curvature a few points cannot rule out. The plan chose that interval and disclosed this; the added bias-aware interval shows how much wider it becomes for stated curvature bounds, and `rdrobust` is not adopted.
- Survey weights change the estimand. The jump is in the weighted mean, a different object from the unweighted jump, which is also reported.

41 provenance records committed in `C03_minimum_wage_staggered/provenance/`.

**Limitations**

- Suppressed county cells. The balanced-panel rule drops every county with a withheld or zero cell in any quarter, and withholding is not random: it is applied where few establishments make a cell identifying. The panel cell prints how many counties are kept, why the rest were dropped, and the share of first-quarter employment in disclosed counties that the kept counties hold. That is not a share of the state totals, because employment in withheld counties is not observed. Every average here is an unweighted county average, and the small rural counties that are most often withheld are the ones missing; the estimates speak to the counties that remain. The retrieval also returns statewide-unknown codes (ending 999) that are not counties; the panel cell prints how many sit in the balanced panel, and a labelled sensitivity drops them.
- Window. The BLS slice API starts in 2014 Q1, so no state has a pre-period before 2014 and the states already above the federal minimum in 2014 have none at all. They are in the naive regression and its decomposition and absent from the interaction-weighted estimate, which therefore speaks only to adopters from 2015 to 2023.
- Dating. The schedule records January-1 values, so an increase made part-way through a year is dated to the next 1 January, and the file is not a consistent January-1 snapshot: some rows carry a rate that took effect part-way through the year. The errors run in both directions. As recalled and not verified in this build, Virginia's 2021 increase took effect on 1 May 2021 and is coded to 2021 Q1, a quarter early; Delaware's 2014 increase took effect in June 2014 and Delaware is coded as above the federal minimum for the whole of 2014, which makes it a treated-throughout state; and the rows for Maryland (2015, 2016, 2018) and California (2014) may show mid-year rates. The pre-period coefficients are the place where dating error would show.

## Synthetic Control

**California SB 3 and Leisure-and-Hospitality Employment.**

`C04_ca_minimum_wage_synth/C04_ca_minimum_wage_synth.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Explain why, with one treated unit, the counterfactual is a weighted average of untreated units, and read the weights as the argument.
- Construct a synthetic control with simplex-constrained weights fitted on the pre-period, and judge its pre-period fit.
- Run permutation inference by treating every donor as the treated unit in turn, and read a rank and a p-value whose smallest possible value depends on the number of donors.
- Test the design with an in-time placebo, a date at which nothing happened.

67 provenance records committed in `C04_ca_minimum_wage_synth/provenance/`.

**Limitations**

- Permutation inference depends on the pool, and has a floor. With 21 donors the smallest possible p-value is 1/22, and the p-value is valid only for the pool as built. The detectability cell prints how large a persistent gap would have to be to reach the plan's threshold, and where California's ratio falls. The estimate is not distinguishable from zero, and the test could not have detected a gap of the size observed.
- The sign of the gap is not stable. The mean gap changes sign across specifications (the predictor-balance cell prints each); two donors carry all the weight, and dropping either changes the answer.
- Convex hull and the predictors. In levels California exceeds every donor in every pre-period month, so the fit uses indexed series. In index space the outcome can be matched, but California's covariates may lie outside the donors' range; the balance table says whether they do. A good pre-period fit says how well the weights did, not that the donors are comparable.

## Matching and Weighting on Observables

**Public Coverage and Employment.**

`C05_public_coverage_matching/C05_public_coverage_matching.ipynb`

**What it shows**

- Compare people only with others who share the same measured characteristics, by stratifying on covariate cells and by weighting with a propensity score, and say what each estimate assumes.
- Check that the adjustment worked, with standardised mean differences before and after weighting and a picture of the overlap between the groups.
- Fit a propensity model to cells of counts, using frequency weights, and see why that equals fitting it to the people the cells stand for.
- Measure how large a further hidden factor would have to be, with Rosenbaum bounds, and read the number Gamma next to the negative control instead of by itself.

**Sources** — American Community Survey Public Use Microdata Sample (acs/acs1/pums)  
12 provenance records committed in `C05_public_coverage_matching/provenance/`.

**Limitations**

- The negative control failed. After adjustment, the share foreign born differs between the groups, and public coverage cannot change where a person was born. The covariate cells therefore leave confounding in place; the cell above this section prints the size. Nativity bears on eligibility (some recent immigrants are barred from some programmes) and on employment, which is what this failure points to. By the plan's own rule the estimates are descriptive associations. Adding nativity to the cells is a labelled sensitivity and does not replace the plan's result.
- The assumption cannot be tested. No unmeasured confounding is the whole claim, and public coverage is plausibly conferred on grounds that bear on work: disability, caregiving, health, state rules. The negative control and the Rosenbaum Gamma say something about it; neither proves it. Gamma is computed on record counts that ignore the survey design and household clustering, which overstates it; the labelled context cell shows how it falls with assumed design effects, and sets it against the measured covariates.
- A selected sample. The income restriction selects on a quantity the outcome moves, as the design section says.
